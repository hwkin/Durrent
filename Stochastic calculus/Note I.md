### 2. Preliminaries

#### 2.1 Finite variation integrals

**Definition** A function $a:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is said to be **càdlàg** if it's right-cts and has left limits. 

<font color='red'>Define integral against nondecreasing càdlàg functions</font>

_Property_ If $a$ is nondecreasing and càdlàg with $a(0)=0$, then there exists a unique Borel measure $da$ on $[0,\infty)$ s.t. $da((s,t])=a(t)-a(s)\forall s<t$.

For $f$ measurable and integrable,one can define **Lebesgue-Stieltjes** integral $f\cdot a$ by $(f\cdot a)(t)=\int_{(0,t]}f(s)da(s)$.

_Property_ $f\cdot a$ is right-cts. When $a$ is cts, $f\cdot a$ is cts. In this case, one can denote $\int_{(0,t]} f(s)da(s)=\int_0^t f(s)da(s)$.

<font color='red'>Define integral against finite variation functions</font>

**Definition** Let $a:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be a càdlàg function. For any $n\in \mathbb N$ and $t\ge 0$, let $v^n(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lceil 2^n t\rceil -1}|a((k+1)2^{-n}-a(k2^{-n}))|$. $v(t)=\lim_{n \to \infty} v^n(t)$ exists for all $t$ and is called **total variation** of 4a$ on $[0,t]$. If $v(t)<\infty$, $a$ is said to have **finite variation on $(0,t]$**. If $v(t)<\infty\forall t$, $a$ is said to have **finite variation**.

**Lemma 2.2** Let $a:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be a càdlàg function of finite variation. Then $v$ is also càdlàg with $\Delta v(t)=|\Delta a(t)|$ ($\Delta a(x)=a(x)-a(x^-)$), and non-decreasing in $t$. In particular, if $a$ ia cts, so is $v$.

**Proposition 2.3**  Let $a:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be a càdlàg function. $a$ is the difference of two right-cts and nondecreasing functions, i.e.,  $a=a^+-a^-$ iff $a$ is of finite variation. 

For $f$ measurable and integrable, one can define **Lebesgue-Stieltjes** integral $f\cdot a$ by $(f\cdot a)(t)=\int_{(0,t]}f(s)da^+(s)-\int_{(0,t]}f(s)da^-(s)$.

#### 2.2 Random integrator

**Definition 2.4** Given a càdlàg adapted process $A$ (i.e., $A(\omega,t)$ is càdlàg for all $\omega\in\Omega$), its **total variation process** $V$ is defined pathwise by letting $V(\omega,\cdot)$ be the total variation of $A(\omega,\cdot)$ for all $\omega\in \Omega$. $A$ is of **finite variation** if $A(\omega,t)$ is for all $\omega\in\Omega$.

**Lemma 2.5** Let $A$ be càdlàg, adapted process of finite variation $V$. Then $V$ is càdlàg, adapted and pathwise nondecreasing. 

**Previsible processes** 

**Definition (discrete-time version)** The process $H_n,n\ge 1$ is **previsible** w.r.t. $\mathcal F_n$ if $H_{n+1}$ is $\mathcal F_n$ measurable for all $n$.

**Definition (continuous-time version)** The **previsible $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal P$ on $\Omega\times (0,\infty)$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by sets of the form $E\times(s,t]$ where $E\in\mathcal F_s,s<t$.A process $H:\Omega\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is said to be previsible if it's measurable w.r.t. $\mathcal P$.

**Example Simple processes** $H(\omega,t)=Z(\omega)\mathbb 1_{(t_1,t_2]}(t)$ where $Z\in\sigma(\mathcal F_{t_1})$ is previsible. Moreover, $H(\omega,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n} Z_k(\omega)\mathbb 1_{(t_k,t_{k+1}]}(t)$ where $Z_k\in\sigma(\mathcal F_{t_k}), 0=t_0<t_1<...<t_{n+1}<\infty$ is previsible. Processes in this form is called **simple**.

_Remark_ All simple processes are left-cts and adapted. 

**Proposition 2.8** Let $X$ be a càdlàg adapted process, and define $H_t:=X_{t^-}$ to be the left-cts modification. Then $H$ is previsible. As a corollary, all cts processes are previsible.

_proof_ Approximate by simple process. Consider $H_t^n=\sum_{k=0}^\infty H_{k2^{-n}}\mathbb 1_{(k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]}(t)$ which is left-cts and previsible. Since $\lim_{n \to \infty} H_t^n=H_t$ a.s. from the left continuity of $H$, $H$ is also previsible. 

**Proposition 2.10** If $H$ is previsible, then $H_t\in \mathcal F_{t^-}$.

_Example_ Poisson process $N_t$ is not previsible since $N_t\in \mathcal F_t$ but $N_t\notin \mathcal F_{t^-}$.

<font color='red'>**Theorem 2.11**</font> Let $A$ be a càdlàg, adapted process of finite variation $V$. Let $H$ be a previsible process and assume that for all $\omega\in\Omega$, $\int_{(0,t]}|H(\omega,s)|dV(\omega,s)<\infty,\forall t>0$. (integrability condition)

Then the process $(H\cdot A):\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ defined by $(H\cdot A)(\omega,t)=\int_{(0,t]}H(\omega,s)dA(\omega,s)$ is càdlàg, adapted process and of finite variation.



### 3. Local martingales

**Definition 3.1** A filtration $(\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge 0}$ is said to satisfy the usual condition if $\mathcal F_0$ contains all $\mathbb P$-null sets and $(\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge 0}$ is right-cts, i.e., $\mathcal F_t=\bigcap_{s>t}\mathcal F_s$. We assume that all filtration will satisfy the usual condition. 

> [!Note]
>
> Recall facts about martingale:
>
> 1. An integrable, adapted process $X$ is a **martingale** w.r.t. $(\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge 0}$ if $\mathbb E(X_t\mid \mathcal F_s)=X_s$ a.s. for $0\le s\le t$. $X$ is a **supermartingale** if $\mathbb E(X_t\mid\mathcal F_s)\le X_s$ a.s. for all $0\le s\le t$ or a **submartingale** of $\mathbb E(X_t\mid \mathcal F_s)\ge X_s$ a.s. for all $0\le s\le t$.
> 2. A r.v. $T$ is a **stopping time** if $\{T\le t \}\in \mathcal F_t\forall t\ge 0$
> 3. If $X$ is càdlàg, let $\mathcal F_T:=\{E\in\mathcal F: E\cap\{T\le t \}\in \mathcal F_t\forall t\ge 0 \}$, then $X_T$ is $\mathcal F_T$-measurable. 
> 4. If $X$ is a martingale, so is the stopped process $X^T:=(X_{t\wedge T})_{t\ge 0}$.

**Theorem 3.2 Optional stopping theorem** Let $X$ be an adapted integrable process, TFAE

(i) $X$ is a martingale

(ii) $X^T$ is a martingale for all stopping times $T$

(iii) For all bounded stopping times $S\le T$, $\mathbb E(X_T\mid \mathcal F_S)=X_S$ a.s.

(iv) For all bounded stopping times $T$, $\mathbb E(X_T)=\mathbb EX_0$.

**Definition 3.3 Local martingale** A càdlàg adapted process is called a **local martingale** if there exists a sequence of stopping times $(T_n)_{n\ge 1}$ with $T_n\uparrow\infty$ a.s., s.t. $X^{T_n}$ is a martingale for all $n$. In this case we say the sequence $T_n$ **reduces** $X$.

_Remark_ Every martingale is a local martingale, since one can pick $T_n=n$. 

> [!Tip]
>
> **Example 3.4** A local martingale may be not a martingale. Let $B$ be a SBM in $\mathbb R^3$, $M_t=1/|B_t|$.
>
> (i) $(M_t)_{t\ge 1}$ is bounded in $L^2$: let $p_t(x)$ be the density of 1-d SBM,  
> $$
> \mathbb E M_t^2=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{1}{x^2+y^2+z^2}p_t(x)p_t(y)p_t(z)dxdydz=\int_{\mathbb R^3}(2\pi t)^{-3/2}\frac{\exp(-\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{2t})}{x^2+y^2+z^2}dxdydz=\int_{\mathbb R^+}8(2\pi t)^{-3/2}2\pi \exp(-r^2/2t)dr=4/t\le 4
> $$
> (ii) $\mathbb EM_t\to 0$ as $t\to\infty$: $\mathbb EM_t\le \sqrt{\mathbb EM_t^2}\to 0$.
>
> (iii) $M_t$ is a supermartingale: Pick $f_\epsilon(x)=(|x|^2+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\in C_b^2(\mathbb R^3)$. Then $f_\epsilon(B_t)-f_\epsilon(B_0)-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^t \Delta f(B_s)ds$ is a martingale, i.e., 
> $$
> \mathbb E[f_\epsilon(B_t)\mid \mathcal F_s]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb E[\int_s^t\Delta f_\epsilon(B_r)dr\mid \mathcal F_s]+f_\epsilon(B_s)
> $$
> Notice $\Delta f_\epsilon=-3\epsilon(|x|^2+\epsilon)^{-5/2}\le 0$. So $E[f_\epsilon(B_t)\mid \mathcal F_s]\le f_\epsilon(B_s)$. Push $\epsilon\to 0$, notice $f_\epsilon(x)$ is decreasing w.r.t. $\epsilon$, so apply MCT and one have $\mathbb E[f(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_s]\le f(B_s)$. 
>
> From (ii) and (iii), $M_t$ is not a martingale.
>
> (iv) $M_t$ is a local martingale. Pick $T_n=\inf\{t\ge 1: |M_t|\ge n \}=\inf\{t\ge 1:|B_t|\le 1/n\}$. 
>
> - $T_n$ is nondecreasing. 
>
> - $M^{T_n}$ is a martingale. If $|B_1|\le 1/n$ we've done. Otherwise pick $f_n\in C_b^2(\mathbb R^3)$ s.t. $f_n=1/|x|$ on $\{ |x|\ge 1/n\}$. Then $f_n(B_t)-f_n(B_0)-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^t \Delta f_n(B_s)ds$ is a martingale. Notice $f(B_{t\wedge T_n})=f_n(B_{t\wedge T_n})$, and $\Delta f_n(B_s)=0$ for $s\le T_n$.  These are enough to show $M^{T_n}$ is a martingale.
>
> - $T_n\uparrow\infty$. Notice $\lim_{n \to \infty} T_n$ exists since $T_n$ is nondecreasing. 
>
>   Let $S_R:=\inf\{t\ge 1: B_t>R\}$. Notice $S_R\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}\infty$. So
>   $$
>   \mathbb P(\lim_{n \to \infty} T_n<\infty)\le\mathbb P(\exists R,\forall n,\lim_{n \to \infty}T_n\le S_R)\le\lim_{R \to \infty}\lim_{n \to \infty}P(T_n\le S_R)=\lim_{R \to \infty}\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{EM_1-1/R}{n-1/R}=0
>   $$

**Proposition 3.5** If $X$ is a nonnegative local martingale, then $X$ is a supermartingale. 

_proof_ by Fatou's lemma, 
$$
\mathbb E(X_t\mid \mathcal F_s)=\mathbb E(\liminf_{n \to \infty}X_{t\wedge T_n}\mid \mathcal F_s)\le\liminf_{n \to \infty} E[X_{t\wedge T_n}\mid \mathcal F_s]=\liminf_{n \to \infty} X_{s\wedge T_n}=X_s
$$
**Definition 3.6 Uniform integrability** A set $\mathcal X$ is said to be **uniformly integrable** if $\sup_{X\in\mathcal X}\mathbb E[|X|\mathbb 1_{|X|>\lambda}]\xrightarrow{\lambda\to\infty}0$.

**Example** If $\mathcal X$ is uniformly bounded or dominated by a nonnegative integrable r.v., $\mathcal X$ is uniformly integrable. 

**Lemma 3.8** If $X\in L^1$, then $\mathcal X:=\{\mathbb E(X\mid \mathcal G): \mathcal G\text{ is a sub }\sigma\text{-algebra} \}$ is uniformly integrable. 

_proof_ Let $Y_\mathcal G=\mathbb E(X\mid\mathcal G)$. Then 
$$
\mathbb E[|Y_\mathcal G|\mathbb 1_{|Y_\mathcal G|\ge \lambda}]\le^1 |\mathbb E[Y_\mathcal G\mathbb 1_{|Y_\mathcal G|\ge\lambda}]|=^2|\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mathbb 1_{|Y_\mathcal G|>\lambda}\mid\mathcal G]]|=|\mathbb E[X\mathbb 1_{|Y_\mathcal G|\ge\lambda}]|
$$
1 comes from Jensen inequality and 2 holds since $\{|Y_\mathcal G|\ge\lambda\}\in\mathcal G$.

Notice $P(|Y_\mathcal G|>\lambda)\le \lambda^{-1}E|Y_\mathcal G|\le \lambda^{-1} E|X|\xrightarrow{\lambda\to\infty}0$. Since $X\in L^1$, $X$ is absolutely continuous, and $|\mathbb E[X\mathbb 1_{|Y_\mathcal G|\ge\lambda}]\xrightarrow{\lambda\to\infty}0$.

**Proposition 3.9** TFAE

(i) $X$ is a martingale

(ii) $X$ is a local martingale and for all $t\ge 0$, the family $\mathcal X_t:=\{X_T:T\text{ is a stopping time with }T\le t \}$ is UI.

_proof_ 

(i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) A martingale must be a local martingale. Notice $X_T=\mathbb E[X_t\mid \mathcal F_T]$, by **Lemma 3.8** $\mathcal X_t$ is UI.

(ii)$\Rightarrow$(i) Suppose $T_n$ reduces $X$. For all bounded stopping times $T\le t$, since $X^{T_n}$ is a martingale, one have 
$$
\mathbb EX_0=\mathbb EX_0^{T_n}=\mathbb EX_T^{T_n}=\mathbb EX_{T\wedge T_n} 
$$
Since $\{X_{T\wedge T_n}\}\subset\mathcal X_t$ is UI, $X_{T\wedge T_n}$ converges a.s. and in $L^1$ to $X_T$, i.e., $EX_0=EX_T$. by optional stopping theorem, $X$ is a martingale.

**Corollary 3.10** A local martingale which is bounded, i.e., $|X_t|\le M\forall t$, or dominated by a nonnegative integrable r.v., i.e., $|X_t|\le Y\forall t$, then $X$ is a martingale.

<font color='red'>**Theorem 3.11**</font> Let $X$ be a cts local martingale with $X_0=0$ and $X$ has finite variation. Then $X\equiv 0$ a.s..

_proof_ Let $V$ be the total variation process. Define the sequence of stopping times $T_n=\inf\{t\ge 0: V_t=n\}$, then $T_n\uparrow\infty$ since $V_t$ is nondecreasing and finite. Moreover, $|X_t^{T_n}|=|X_{t\wedge T_n}|\le |V_{t\wedge T_n}|\le n$, i.e., $X^{T_n}$ is bounded. Suppose $S_n$ reduces $X$, we have $(X^{T_n})^{S_m}_t=X_{t\wedge T_n\wedge S_m}=(X^{S_m})^{T_n}_t$, so $X^{T_n}$ is a local martingale reduced by $S_m$. By **Corollary 3.10** $X^{T_n}$ is a martingale. So $T_n$ reduces $X$.

To show $X\equiv 0$ it suffices to show $X^{T_n}\equiv 0\forall n$, say $Y=X^{T_n}$. $Y$ is a cts and bdd martingale. To show $Y\equiv 0$ it suffices to show $\mathbb EY_t^2=0\forall t$. Fix $t>0$, for $N\ge 1$ write $t_k=tk/N$, 
$$
\mathbb E Y_t^2=\mathbb E(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(Y_{t_{k+1}}^2-Y_{t_k}^2))=\mathbb E(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k})^2)\le\mathbb E(\sup_{0\le k<N}|Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k}|\cdot \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k}|)
$$
Notice $|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} |Y_{t_{k+1}-Y_{t_k}}|\le V_t^{T_n}\le n$, and $\sup_{0\le k<N}|Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k}|\le V_t^{T_n}\le n$. Since $Y$ is cts, $\sup_{0\le k<N}|Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_k}|\xrightarrow{N\to\infty} 0$. So by BCT, push $N\to\infty$ we have $EY_t^2=0$. 

_Remark_ The continuity condition cannot be removed. 

**Proposition 3.13** Let $X$ be a cts local martingale with $X_0=0$. Then the sequence of stopping times $T_n:=\inf\{t>0: |X_t|=n\}$ reduces $X$. 

_proof_ Mimic that of **Theorem 3.11**. 

_Remark_ This is an explicit reducing sequence for all cts local martingales despite not knowing the reducing sequence from which it's defined. 

### 4. The stochastic integral

**Theorem 4.1 Martingale convergence theorem** Let $X$ be càdlàg and $L^2$-bdd martingale. Then there exists a r.v. $X_\infty$ s.t. $X_t\to X_\infty$ a.s. and in $L^2$. Moreover, $X_t=\mathbb E(X_\infty\mid\mathcal F_t)$. 

**Theorem 4.2 Doob's $L^2$ inequality** Let $X$ be a càdlàg and $L^2$-bdd martingale. Then $\mathbb E\sup_{t\ge 0}|X_t|^2\le4\mathbb EX_\infty^2$.

_Notation_ $\mathcal M^2$ is the collection of $L^2$-bdd càdlàg martingales, $\mathcal M_c^2$ is the collection of $L^2$-bdd cts martingales and $\mathcal M_{c,loc}^2$ is the collection of $L^2$-bdd cts local martingales.

**Definition 4.3 Simple process** A process $H$ is a **simple process** if $H(\omega,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} Z_k(\omega)\mathbb 1_{(t_k,t_{k+1}]}(t)$ for $0=t_0<t_1<...<t_n<\infty$ and $Z_k$ bdd measurable w.r.t. $\mathcal F_k$. Let $\mathcal S$ be the collection of simple processes.

<font color='red'>Define integrals of $\mathcal S$ against $\mathcal M^2$ </font> 

#### 4.1 Simple integrands

**Definition 4.4** For $H_t=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} Z_k \mathbb 1_{(t_k,t_{k+1}]}(t)$ simple process and $M\in \mathcal M^2$, set $(H\cdot M)_t:=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} Z_k(M_{t_{k+1}\wedge t}-M_{t_k\wedge t})$. 

**Proposition 4.5** If $H\in\mathcal S$ and $M\in\mathcal M^2$, then $H\cdot M\in\mathcal M^2$. Moreover, $E[(H\cdot M)_\infty^2]=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E[Z_k^2(M_{t_{k+1}-M_{t_k}})^2]\le 4\|H\|_\infty \mathbb E[(M_\infty-M_0)^2]$.

_proof_ One can verify $(H\cdot M)$ is a martingale, and its $L^2$ norm is given by 
$$
E[(H\cdot M)_t^2]=\mathbb E[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} Z_k(M_{t_{k+1}\wedge t}-M_{t_k\wedge t})]^2=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E[Z_k(M_{t_{k+1}\wedge t}-M_{t_k\wedge t})]^2\le \|H\|_\infty^2\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E(M_{t_{k+1}\wedge t}-M_{t_k\wedge t})^2=\|H\|_\infty^2\mathbb E[M_{t_n\wedge t}-M_0]^2\le \|H\|_\infty^24\mathbb E(M_\infty-M_0)^2<\infty
$$
So $(H\cdot M)\in \mathcal M^2$.

**Proposition 4.6** Let $H\in\mathcal S$ and $M\in\mathcal M^2$. Then for any stopping time $T$, $H\cdot M^T=(H\cdot M)^T$.

#### 4.2 the space of integrators

For $X$ càdlàg adapted process, define $\left\vert\!\left\vert\!\left\vert X\right\vert\!\right\vert\!\right\vert=\|X^*\|_2$, where $X^*=\sup_{t\ge 0}|X_t|$. Let $\mathcal C^2=\{X: \left\vert\!\left\vert\!\left\vert X\right\vert\!\right\vert\!\right\vert<\infty \}$. Then $\left\vert\!\left\vert\!\left\vert\cdot\right\vert\!\right\vert\!\right\vert$ is a norm on $\mathcal C^2$.

For $X\in\mathcal M^2$, define $\|X\|:=\|X_\infty\|_2$. This is a norm on $\mathcal M^2$. 

> It suffices to verify $\|X\|=0\Rightarrow X_t=0\forall t$ a.s.. Notice $X_t=E[X_\infty\mid\mathcal F_t]$, so $\|X\|=0\Rightarrow \|X_\infty\|=0\Rightarrow X_t=0\forall t$.

_Notation_ $\mathcal M$ is the collection of càdlàg martingales, $\mathcal M_c$ is the collection of cts martingales, and $M_{c,loc}$ is the collection of cts local martingales.

**Proposition 4.7**

(i) $(\mathcal C^2,\left\vert\!\left\vert\!\left\vert\cdot \right\vert\!\right\vert\!\right\vert)$ is complete.

(ii) $\mathcal M^2=\mathcal M\cap \mathcal C^2$

(iii) $(\mathcal M^2,\|\cdot\|)$ is a Hilbert space and $\mathcal M_c^2=\mathcal M_c\cap \mathcal M^2$ is a closed subspace. 

(iv) The map $\mathcal M^2\to L^2(\mathcal F_\infty)$ given by $X\mapsto X_\infty$ is an isometry.
